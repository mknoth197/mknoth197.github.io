I'm a week into a new team at work. The charter, briefly: figure out what effective AI usage looks like across the enterprise, and help standardize it so that "the teams getting real value from AI" stops being tribal knowledge.

The first thing I keep bumping into is that almost every claim about AI productivity right now is a vibe. "Claude wrote half my PR." "Cursor made me 3x faster." "I shipped a feature over the weekend." These are interesting anecdotes, but they're not measurements. They don't tell me whether the practice generalizes, whether it holds up under load, or whether the teams that aren't experiencing those gains are missing a skill, a tool, or a piece of context.

If we're going to treat AI as a first-class participant in the SDLC — something I increasingly believe we have to — then we need the same kind of honest measurement we'd apply to anything else that touches production.

## The question isn't "does AI help"

At this point, the question "does AI help engineers" is settled enough that I'm not interested in re-litigating it. The question that matters is: **where, specifically, does AI help, by how much, and under what conditions?**

A few things I want to start measuring, concretely:

- Time-to-first-working-draft on a given task, with and without AI in the loop.
- Review cycles per PR — does AI-assisted work need more review passes, fewer, or the same?
- Defect rates downstream. An AI-drafted change that ships fast but breaks twice isn't a win.
- The distribution of where time is saved. Is it in code? In writing? In reading unfamiliar systems? In generating tests?

None of these are radical metrics. They're just disciplined ones. The discipline is in separating them from "how did that feel."

## Context is the missing input

The more I sit with this, the more convinced I am that the real variable isn't the model — it's the context available to the model.

Karpathy's riff on LLM wikis pointed at something that matches my lived experience: AI output gets dramatically better when it has access to the kind of knowledge that normally lives in a senior engineer's head. How a given team names things. Which internal library to prefer over the obvious open-source one. Which database is soft-deprecated. Which code paths are load-bearing and which are scaffolding nobody's cleaned up yet.

I've been calling this "pet knowledge" — the organizational intuition that every tenured engineer accumulates and that is almost never written down. You know it when someone new joins the team and asks a question that seems obvious until you realize: we literally have never written the answer anywhere.

If that's true — and I think it increasingly is — then the bottleneck for enterprise AI adoption isn't prompt quality or model choice. It's **context infrastructure**: the systems we build to make implicit team knowledge explicit and machine-readable.

## What I'm going to try next

A few experiments I'm planning to run on my own team first, before trying to generalize:

1. **Baseline.** Pick a few realistic tasks I'd do this week. Do them both ways — with and without AI — and actually time them. Note friction points honestly, including the ones where AI made things slower.
2. **Context audit.** For one real task where AI underperformed, ask: what would I have needed to give it to make it work? Write that down. Then check whether that thing exists anywhere in our docs.
3. **Wiki seed.** Start capturing pet knowledge in a form an LLM can read. Not a Confluence page that'll go stale — something versioned, close to the code, and updated as part of the work.

I don't know what I'll find. I expect some of my assumptions to survive contact with the data and some not to. That's the point.

The goal isn't to produce a conference talk with a clean narrative. It's to replace vibes with measurements, one small experiment at a time — and see what that lets us standardize.
